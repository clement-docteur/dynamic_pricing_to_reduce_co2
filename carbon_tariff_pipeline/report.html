15.095 Machine Learning Under a Modern Optimization Lens · project results

Carbon-aware tariffs for London households

We ran the full proposal on Low Carbon London 2013: a next-day carbon-intensity forecast, marginal emission factors for the 2013 grid, a demand-response simulator checked against held-out tariff events, and an optimal policy tree that picks each day's tariff schedule. Counting emissions at the margin, the tree beats the 2013 time-of-use schedule by about one percent of household emissions, and it raises bills.

Research question

Can an interpretable, carbon-aware pricing policy cut household emissions at a comparable average price?

Yes, slightly. Counting emissions with marginal emission factors (the plants that actually ramp when demand changes), a depth-1 policy tree lowers the estimated emissions of the 1,032 trial households by 0.90% of their footprint over the unseen test period, Nov-Dec 2013 (95% simulator bootstrap: 0.80% to 1.01%), about 2.7 kg CO2 per household over 61 days. Its time-average price is 13.58 p/kWh against a target of 13.61. Perfect hindsight saves 0.97%, so with this menu and the response the trial households actually showed, the ceiling is about one percent. Held to the 2013 tariff's own test-period average price (12.80 p/kWh), the tree still saves 0.71% with a mean bill change of +0.6%.

Nov-Dec is the best case, and a budget-aware tree recovers the rest. Repeating the whole protocol on four 2-month test windows from May to December, at the 2013 tariff's own price in each window, the tree's change averages −0.37% (from +0.10% to −0.71%) against a hindsight ceiling of −0.62%: in May-Jun and Jul-Aug its seasonal rule switches events off entirely. Giving the tree one more input, the remaining price budget per remaining day, lifts the four-window average to −0.59%, close to the ceiling. The budget-aware tree spends unused budget in summer and paces itself in winter: in Nov-Dec it saves 0.93% and the price rule overrides it on 6 days instead of 45. It also spends more of the budget, so the mean bill rises by +14.5%.

Marginal accounting changes the size, not the policy. At the margin, overnight power in 2013 came from coal (about 679 g/kWh), while the evening peak was met by gas and pumped storage. Once storage output is charged with the overnight coal that refilled it, the evening peak runs at about 514 g/kWh, above the average intensity of those hours (462). So the tree's evening conservation is worth a little more than average accounting says (0.90% vs 0.76%), and the best policy is still an evening High block. The policy that suffers is a carbon-only rule that ignores demand response: following the marginal factors, it puts High prices at 02:30, when almost nobody uses power, and Low prices at 12:00-17:00, and it raises emissions by 1.68% (following average intensity: +0.18%).

The saving comes from conservation, not timing. High prices cut evening use without moving it elsewhere, and the evening peak is when use is highest. Direct optimisation picks exactly the same schedules, so interpretability costs nothing here. A perfect forecast of the marginal factors changes direct optimisation by 0.00 points.

A time-average price cap does not protect bills. High blocks fall on the evening peak, so at the main target the average price actually paid rises from 12.99 to 14.30 p/kWh and the mean bill by +9.2%. Capping the average price paid at its Jan-Oct historical level (13.78 p/kWh) limits the increase to +4.0% while still saving 0.78%.

The size depends on the simulator. Under the CVAE simulator, which barely reacts to High prices on held-out days, the main tree's saving falls to 0.01%; a tree re-trained on the CVAE saves 0.49%.

Data and protocol

What was used, and when each piece is allowed to look

  • Households: 1,032 Low Carbon London households on the 2013 dynamic tariff with >95% of 2013 readings (gaps filled), grouped into the four consumption clusters of the CVAE reproduction (285, 299, 299, 149 households), plus 1,032 flat-tariff (Std) households used as a control. Cluster 3 (149 households) carries most of the price response.
  • Tariff: the authors' 2013 schedule (aligned to the meter half-hours; Tariffs.xlsx is wrong on 17 late-2013 days).
  • Grid: NESO half-hourly carbon intensity and generation mix, 2009-2013 (the 2013 grid is coal-heavy, mean 475 g/kWh). Weather: ERA5 2 m temperature at central London, 2013.
  • Emissions: marginal emission factors by hour and season (Step 1b), with pumped storage charged at the overnight marginal factor. Changes are reported as a share of the households' historical footprint at average intensity.
  • Timing: the tariff for day d is announced at 12:00 GMT on d−1, so every forecast and policy input uses data up to that moment (CI horizon 12-36 h). Realised temperature stands in for a day-ahead forecast.
  • Splits: train Jan-Aug 2013 (243 days), validation Sep-Oct (61), test Nov-Dec (61). Models, the schedule menu, tree depth and the price multiplier are chosen on train and validation only; the test period is used once, at the end.
  • Price target: the historical TOU time-average price over Jan-Oct, 13.608 p/kWh. The historical tariff averaged only 12.795 p/kWh in the test period, so a price-matched variant is reported too.
Step 1

Forecasting next-day carbon intensity

The day-ahead forecast of average carbon intensity (CI) feeds the policy tree's features and the average-accounting comparison. Each model predicts the deviation of every half-hour's CI from the mean of the last known 24 h, from calendar features, lagged CI (latest known day, 2 and 7 days earlier), lagged fuel shares and lagged generation. Training uses NESO data from 2009, refitted monthly. On validation the tree ensembles cut the error of persistence by 24% (24.0 vs 31.6 g/kWh), and XGBoost was selected. On the test period the gain shrinks: XGBoost 27.2, random forest 26.0, persistence 28.4 g/kWh. All models rank the half-hours within a day poorly (rank correlation 0.32 for XGBoost vs 0.38 for persistence), most likely because the swings follow wind output and plant availability, which lagged data do not anticipate. Adding temperature does not help: validation MAE 25.4 → 25.2, test 27.7 → 28.0 g/kWh (2013-only training, as ERA5 covers 2013 only).

Step 1b

Marginal emission factors: which plants respond to demand

A tariff changes demand at the margin, so its emissions depend on the plants that ramp, not on the average mix. For each hour of day and season we regress the half-hourly change in system emissions on the change in generation (the method of Hawkes, 2010). NESO counts pumped-storage output as zero-carbon, but that energy was pumped overnight; in the main factors, storage output carries the overnight marginal factor divided by a 75% round-trip efficiency (930 g/kWh). Factors used to score schedules are estimated on 2013; the factors available at decision time are re-estimated each month on the previous 12 months (test-period error 18 g/kWh).

The 2013 margin looks nothing like the average. Overnight, coal met 58% of changes in generation and the marginal factor is about 679 g/kWh. At the evening peak, gas met 64% and pumped storage 17%: about 318 g/kWh if storage is counted as zero-carbon, 514 once it is charged for its overnight coal. Over the test period the factors average 548 g/kWh (unadjusted 461), against an average intensity of 462. The margin also moved between years, so factors from 2009-2012 would have overstated 2013.

Step 2

Simulating demand under other tariffs

The proposal planned to adapt Brégère and Bessa's CVAE. Our reproduction found its mean profiles accurate but its tariff response unreliable: the latent variable collapses, the response changes sign between training runs, and its "side effects" around price blocks are not in the data. We therefore use it only as a sensitivity case. The main simulator is a response model: for each cluster, the log ratio of ToU to flat-tariff consumption is regressed on slot × day-type intercepts, temperature, and tariff terms (in-block High and Low effects by time of day, and edge effects). Flat-tariff households share the weather, holidays and season, so these cancel out. A schedule's simulated consumption is the observed consumption times the modelled ratio between that schedule and the historical one; only the tariff response is modelled.

Three specifications were compared on Sep-Oct event days; the one with only the edge effects that the reproduction had verified wins (2 h dip before Low blocks, the half-hour after a Low block, 2 h before High blocks). Inside blocks, cluster 3 cuts use by 29% under evening High prices and raises it by 49% under afternoon Low prices; in the other three clusters no in-block effect exceeds 6%.

Step 3

Learning an interpretable tariff policy

Each day's action is one schedule from a small menu. Its model-based cost is the change in emissions, Cd(z) = Σt MEFdt · ŷdt(z) / 1000 kg CO2, with the 2013 marginal factors and simulated consumption on the fitting days; the direct optimiser and the carbon-only rule use the decision-time factors instead. The policy sees only decision-time features (CI forecast summaries, temperature, weekend or holiday, recent consumption). The price constraint enters as a Lagrangian penalty λ · price, and the proposal's feasibility rule keeps the test-period average attainable: a day keeps its recommendation only if the target could still be met with every remaining day on the Normal tariff; otherwise it falls back to the next affordable schedule in the leaf's ranking. The tree is an exact optimal prescriptive tree (Lecture 11) of depth 1-3; depth and λ were chosen on validation, and the tree was refitted on Jan-Oct.

No carbon-forecast feature enters the tree: in the training data, how much electricity households were using mattered more than the day's carbon intensity.

A budget-aware tree

The tree above cannot see how much of the price budget is left, so it asks for a High block every winter day and the price rule decides which days get one. The budget-aware tree has one more input: the remaining budget per remaining day, i.e. how much the average daily price can still exceed an all-Normal day (11.76 p/kWh) without breaking the target. It is computed each day from the prices already announced. To train it, every fitting day is repeated at 9 budget levels from 0 to 9.5 p/kWh. Each level is priced with the λ that would make the policy spend that much per day on the fitting days (from 0.0133 at zero budget to 0.0000 at the top). A budget-aware direct optimiser uses the same mapping. Depth 2 was chosen on validation.

Marginal vs average

What changes when emissions are counted at the margin

  • The policy barely changes. Trained on average intensity, the tree asks for High 16:00-20:00 and, scored with marginal factors, saves 0.87%; trained on marginal factors it saves 0.90%. Both are evening conservation policies.
  • The measured saving is a little larger (0.90% vs 0.76%), because the storage-adjusted evening margin is dirtier than the evening average.
  • Pumped storage decides the sign of that difference. Counting storage as zero-carbon, as NESO's intensity does, makes the evening peak look clean (318 g/kWh) and shrinks the tree's saving to 0.65%.
  • The carbon-only rule is the policy most affected. Following marginal factors, it moves its High block to the night (02:30), when consumption is lowest, and its Low block to 12:00-17:00, when far more power is used. It goes from +0.18% to +1.68%: placing prices by carbon intensity alone, without a demand model, can backfire.

Rolling evaluation

Does the result hold outside one winter?

The main test covers 61 winter days. To see how much rests on that window, we repeat the whole protocol on four 2-month test windows (May-Jun, Jul-Aug, Sep-Oct, Nov-Dec). For each window, the response model is refitted on the days before it, the menu is chosen on the training days, and depth and λ are chosen on the 2 months just before the window, so no window sees its own data. Early windows have short histories (59 training days for May-Jun).

  • Nov-Dec is the best window. At each window's own target the tree's change is May-Jun +0.10%, Jul-Aug −0.32%, Sep-Oct −0.57%, Nov-Dec −0.90% (mean −0.42%). The targets differ from what the 2013 tariff actually charged, so the price-matched comparison is the fairer one: there the tree averages −0.37% and perfect hindsight −0.62%.
  • In summer the tree does nothing. In May-Jun and Jul-Aug the tree never prescribes an event. Its threshold on recent consumption is learned on the colder months before each window, so every summer day falls on the "Normal" side. The tree then only gains from dropping the 2013 tariff's events, and in May-Jun, when the 2013 tariff ran many High blocks (15.00 p/kWh on average), it loses to it. Hindsight shows that summer High blocks would still have saved emissions.
  • The budget-aware tree uses that summer budget. Price-matched, it has events on 42 days in May-Jun, 6 days in Jul-Aug, 23 days in Sep-Oct, 55 days in Nov-Dec, and its change is May-Jun −0.64%, Jul-Aug −0.42%, Sep-Oct −0.58%, Nov-Dec −0.73% (mean −0.59%, against −0.62% for hindsight).
  • Direct optimisation matches the tree in 3 of 4 windows, so the cost of interpretability stays small throughout.
  • The carbon-only rule raises emissions in every window (+0.92% to +2.14%).

Sensitivity

How much the answer depends on the price cap and the simulator

  • Price-matched to the historical test period (12.795 p/kWh): the tree's change is −0.71% and direct optimisation's −0.71%. So 22% of the main result comes from spending a larger price budget than the 2013 tariff did in Nov-Dec. At this target the tree's mean bill changes by +0.6%.
  • Flat-tariff cap (14.228 p/kWh): tree −1.06%, oracle −1.13%. A larger budget buys more High blocks and more conservation.
  • Cap on the average price paid (≤ 13.78 p/kWh, the Jan-Oct historical value): tree −0.78% with mean bill +4.0%. Bills still rise because the 2013 tariff charged only 12.99 p/kWh in Nov-Dec; a cap at that level would be bill-neutral by construction.
  • Demand response: halving the response or scaling it by 1.5 scales the savings almost proportionally (−0.49% and −1.25% for the tree). Dropping the edge effects changes little; the full model, with post-block and day-level terms, gives −1.16% for a re-trained tree.
  • CVAE simulator: the main tree's saving falls to −0.01%: the CVAE barely responds to High prices on held-out days (Step 2). Re-trained on the CVAE, the tree reaches −0.49% and the hindsight oracle −0.76%.

Solver check

Would a stronger tree solver change the answer?

The proposal planned Interpretable AI's Optimal Policy Trees (OPT). Our trees come from our own search, which tries every threshold at the last split but only 12 quantile thresholds at the splits above it. If that shortcut missed better trees, a stronger solver such as OPT could find them. We refitted every Step 3 tree (4 test windows, fixed and budget-aware, depths 1-3: 24 fits) on its own training set with a finer search: every threshold at every level for depths 1 and 2, which gives the true optimum, and 24 thresholds above the last level for depth 3 (the exhaustive depth-3 search takes hours). The refits with our original search reproduce Step 3's trees and test results exactly.

  • Depth 1 is already exact. Of the 8 trees chosen in Step 3, 4 have depth 1, including the main Nov-Dec tree, so no solver can improve them.
  • At depth 2 our search gets 98.3-100% of the optimum's training gain (gain = cost of the best single schedule for every day minus the tree's cost). At depth 3 it gets 98.0-100.2% of the finer search's. The finer grid is not a superset of ours, so it can do slightly worse.
  • The extra training gain does not carry over to the test windows. At the depths chosen in Step 3 the finer trees give the same test result in 6 of 8 cases, and slightly smaller savings in the others (Sep-Oct tree: −0.555% vs −0.573%; Nov-Dec budget-aware tree: −0.933% vs −0.935%).
  • Interpretable AI itself could not run on this PC. IAI 3.2.2 and its licence are installed, but Windows Smart App Control blocks libraries bundled with its system image. 06_tree_check.py --export writes the 24 training sets, and 06_iai_fit.jl fits OPT on them (same depth, minimum leaf size 10, no complexity penalty, so only the search differs) on any machine where IAI runs. Changes from the proposal

Where we departed from the plan, and why

  • Emission metric. The proposal priced emissions at the average carbon intensity. A tariff changes demand at the margin, so the main results use marginal emission factors (storage-adjusted); average intensity is kept as a comparison.
  • Main simulator. The CVAE is kept as a sensitivity case; the main simulator is a cluster-level response model with a flat-tariff control, because the reproduction showed the CVAE's response to schedule changes is unreliable and the response model matches held-out tariff events.
  • Action costs use measured factors on the fitting days. Emission factors are not affected by the tariff, so the tree learns the mapping from forecasts to schedules against the outcomes it will face; the direct optimiser uses decision-time factors and forecasts, as planned.
  • Lagrangian multiplier. The average-price constraint is priced into each action's cost; λ is a hyperparameter chosen with the tree depth on validation. Tuning λ to hit the target on the training days made coarse trees spend nothing on the validation period.
  • Feasibility rule floor. "Attainable" is checked assuming every remaining day could be an all-Normal day, so once the budget is spent the rule cancels events. Using the cheapest schedule (a night Low block) as the floor instead let policies overspend early and then forced Low days, which add consumption.
  • Budget-aware tree. Added after the rolling evaluation showed the original tree switching events off in summer: the remaining budget per remaining day becomes a tree input, trained over a grid of budget levels.
  • Extra rows we added: direct optimisation with a perfect carbon forecast (value of better forecasts), a hindsight oracle (ceiling), a target matched to the historical test-period price, and a cap on the average price paid.
  • Implementation. Interpretable AI's Optimal Policy Trees are installed but blocked on this PC (Smart App Control). The tree is our own search over splits of depth ≤ 3 (every threshold at the last level, 12 quantile thresholds above). The solver check shows that a finer search does not improve the test results.
Limitations

What these numbers can and cannot say

  • All counterfactual emissions are model-based. The response model reproduces observed in-block and edge effects on held-out days, but new schedules are combinations of effects estimated from 2013 events.
  • Marginal factors are short-run statistical estimates (hour × season regressions on system-level changes). They ignore transmission losses, interconnector effects beyond NESO's import accounting and any long-run effect on what gets built; the storage adjustment assumes a 75% round-trip efficiency and overnight pumping.
  • Each test window is 2 months long, and the early rolling windows have short training histories. The bootstrap intervals cover the main window only.
  • Households are represented by four clusters; bill changes assume each household responds like its cluster.
  • Weather enters as realised temperature (a perfect day-ahead forecast); wind speed, a major driver of CI swings, is not in the data.
Reproduce

Files and commands

Run from carbon_tariff_pipeline/ with python -P, in order (about 20 minutes in total; Step 1 takes about 5, each Step 3 run about 6). Step 3 runs twice: --metric marginal (main) and --metric average. The solver check runs after Step 3 and adds about 13 minutes. The pipeline reuses the reproduction's household data, clusters and trained CVAEs from CVAE_reproduction/.
